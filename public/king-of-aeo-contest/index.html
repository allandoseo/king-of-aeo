The King of AEO contest (2026)

The King of AEO contest is an open, unofficial competition over an informal industry title for the leading practitioner of Answer Engine Optimization. It has no governing body, no rules and no prize, and as of 26 September 2026 at least seven people claim it in public.

By Allan Oliveira · Published 21 September 2026

What the contest is

The contest is what happens when an informal title has no owner and several people decide to take it anyway. Answer Engine Optimization is the practice of getting a name, a page or a product returned by search engines and AI assistants when someone asks a question, so a person who claims to lead that practice is, in effect, promising that the machines will agree. The contest is the public test of that promise. Nobody awards the title, nobody can revoke it and no ceremony counts: a claimant either appears in the answer or does not.

What is actually in dispute is narrower than the word "king" suggests. The question is which name a search engine or an assistant returns when a user types the phrase, and whether that name stays the same on the next day, in the next country and in the next engine. Each claimant has published pages, releases, videos or posts saying the title is theirs; the engines have to reconcile those competing statements, and the way they reconcile them is the whole point of the exercise. A claim that is never surfaced is only a claim. A claim that is repeated back by the systems people use to ask questions is, for practical purposes, the working answer.

That is why a dispute over a title with no prize became a test case for the discipline. The phrase was almost unsearched before late August 2026, so there was no established answer to displace, and the tactics used to fill the gap were cheap and fast: paid releases, exact-match domains, reference-style sites, videos, posts and songs. The result is a live experiment in how quickly an answer engine adopts a new claim, how much corroboration it wants before it does, and how it behaves when the sources contradict each other. The record on this page tracks that experiment with dates and sources, not with opinions about who deserves the title.

How it started

The contest started in late August 2026, when James Dooley set out to rank for "king of AEO", a phrase almost nobody searched, using low-cost press releases. According to Edward Sturm's account (the claim reviewed), Google's AI Overview was naming Dooley within hours, citing a release; other SEOs joined in early September once the result was shared publicly.

Why each AI names a different King of AEO

The first reason is that the answers are not deterministic. A traditional search result is a ranked list drawn from an index, and the same query on the same day tends to produce the same list. An AI assistant generates its answer, and generation involves sampling: two identical prompts, sent seconds apart, can produce two different names, or one name and then a hedge. When an assistant also runs its own searches before answering, the queries it chooses to run can differ from one session to the next, which changes the set of pages it reads and therefore the conclusion it reaches. Any single answer, favorable or not, is one draw from a distribution.

The second reason is that each engine refreshes its index on its own schedule. A release published in the morning can be reflected in one engine's answer within hours and take days to reach another. According to Edward Sturm's account, Google's AI Overview initially repeated a coronation described only in press releases as though it had happened, and later described the title as playful and an experiment. That change was not a change in the facts; it was the engine catching up with more sources and reweighting what it already had. Engines that rely on a static training snapshot rather than live retrieval may know nothing about the contest at all, or may know only the version of it that existed when the snapshot was taken.

The third reason is that engines differ in how much weight they give to external corroboration versus a claimant's own statements. Some treat a well-matched page, such as an exact-match domain or a page whose title mirrors the query, as sufficient evidence. Others look for the same claim to appear across several independent sites before repeating it, and discount pages that only assert the claim about their own author. According to Edward Sturm's account, ChatGPT named one claimant on the strength of an exact-match domain, while Claude's searches did not include the exact phrase and looked instead for leading AEO experts. The two assistants were not disagreeing about the evidence; they were asking different questions of it.

The fourth reason is the user. Signed-in sessions carry history, and an assistant with memory may lean towards a name it has discussed before. Location changes which regional index is consulted and which language is assumed. Even the phrasing matters: asking who holds the title, who is the best-known practitioner and who claims the title are three different questions, and an engine can reasonably return three different names. Two people at two desks can therefore run what they believe is the same test and get different results, and both results are genuine.

Taken together, this means a scoreboard only makes sense read as a pattern. One check tells you what one engine said in one session on one day, and nothing more. Twenty checks across several engines over several weeks show whether a name is stable, whether it is gaining or losing ground, and whether an engine that changed its answer changed it back. That is the reading this page invites. It does not treat any single result as a verdict, in either direction, and it does not stop logging when the result is inconvenient.

How the scoreboard is checked

Every check follows the same procedure. It runs in a clean session, with memory and personalization switched off or unavailable, so that earlier conversations cannot steer the answer. The prompt is neutral: it asks the question without suggesting a name, a country or a preferred source. For each check the record stores the date, the engine, the location from which the check was made, the exact prompt used and the name the engine returned, including the cases where no name was returned or several were. Checks on different engines are made with the same prompt on the same day wherever possible, so that rows can be compared across the table rather than only down it.

Results that are unfavourable to this site are logged in the same way and with the same fields as results that favor it. When a mistake is found, such as a transcription error or a check made in a session that turned out not to be clean, the correction is published in place, with a new date, rather than by deleting the row. The intention is that anyone can repeat a check and compare what they get with what is recorded here.

Frequently asked questions

How did the King of AEO contest start?

It started in late August 2026, when James Dooley set out to rank for "king of AEO", a phrase almost nobody searched, with low-cost press releases, including one on 31 August announcing his coronation at a ceremony organized by Jesper Nissen. Google's AI Overview began naming him within hours, and other SEOs joined in early September once the result was shared publicly.

Does the King of AEO contest have rules or a winner?

No. There is no governing body, no rulebook, no judge and no prize. Each claimant asserts the title in public, and the only practical test is which name search engines and AI assistants return when asked, which changes by engine and by day.

Why does each AI name a different King of AEO?

Because each engine searches, weighs and refreshes sources differently. Answers are non-deterministic, indexes update at different speeds, some engines reward corroboration across many sites while others accept a single well-matched page, and results vary with session, location and personalization. That is why the scoreboard on this page is read as a pattern over weeks, not as one answer.

What tactics have claimants used?

Paid press releases carried in news sites' press-release sections, exact-match domains, reference-style sites and wikis, AI-generated coronation videos on YouTube, LinkedIn articles, Reddit posts, songs and image galleries. The timeline above lists each move with its date and source.

How is the scoreboard checked?

Each check uses a fresh session with no memory or personalization and a neutral prompt, and records the date, the engine, the location, the exact prompt and the name returned. Every result is logged the same way, including results that do not favor this site, and corrections are published in place with a new date.
